Non-line-of-sight wireless link

A wireless link between two houses on the same street that had worked for years, until it stopped. I brought it back, used measurements to find the bottleneck and tuned it without replacing any hardware.

The problem

The fibre connection is in one house (A) and reaches another house on the same street (B), about 60 metres away, by radio. The antennas have no line of sight: the street curves slightly and the front of house A is set back behind a porch.

In summer 2026 the link went down. The cable from the antenna to the router in house B was an indoor cable and the weather had destroyed it. When fixing it, nobody remembered the devices' passwords and both houses had new routers, so everything had to be set up again.

The network

Both antennas work in bridge mode and the routers in house B are set up as access points. It's a single network: a phone in house B gets its address from the router in house A, as if it were there.

DeviceModelRole
Antennas2 × Ubiquiti NanoStation Loco M22.4 GHz bridge link, WPA2-AES, airMAX
House AISP fibre routerInternet access and address assignment
House BASUS RT-AC51UDual-band access point
CablingOutdoor Cat 6 UTPReplaces the indoor cable that failed

Transmit power is 12 dBm. With the antenna's 8 dBi gain that gives 20 dBm of radiated power, exactly the legal limit in Spain for 2.4 GHz. That's why I didn't raise the power to make up for the lack of line of sight.

Diagnosis

With the link working again, house B got about 11 Mbps while house A got over 500. Before changing anything, I measured the speed at every point of the network to see what was holding it back.

Where I measuredDownloadUpload
House A, 5 GHz Wi-Fi587 Mbps361 Mbps
House B, 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi11 Mbps16 Mbps
House B, cable11 Mbps15 Mbps
House B, 5 GHz Wi-Fi10 Mbps16 Mbps

In house B it made no difference whether I used a cable or Wi-Fi, so the routers weren't the problem: the radio link was. The antennas' link quality indicator (CCQ) was around 50 %, meaning many packets were lost and had to be resent.

Re-aiming the antenna in house B changed nothing. That makes sense: with no line of sight the signal arrives reflected from several directions, not from one. What was left was interference: there are over 20 Wi-Fi networks in the same band around.

Tuning

The link was on channel 1, which looked like a good choice on paper. I tried channel 6 using airOS test mode, which applies a change for a few minutes and rolls it back unless you confirm it. That way there was no risk of leaving house B offline.

The weaker direction went from 4 to 17 Mbps and the link became balanced. On channel 1 the results also jumped a lot from one test to the next; on channel 6 they are stable. Interestingly, channel 6 had more neighbouring networks than channel 1, but weaker ones: channel 1 overlapped with a strong network on channel 2.

Day to day

In house B, wired download speed went from 11.3 to 17.7 Mbps on average. I then set the access point's 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi to channel 11 at 20 MHz width, so it wouldn't clash with the antenna right next to it, and Wi-Fi started performing like the cable, with half the latency.

House BBeforeAfter
Wired download11.3 Mbps17.7 Mbps
2.4 GHz Wi-Fi download12.9 Mbps19.5 Mbps
2.4 GHz Wi-Fi latency89 ms27 ms

A 2.4 GHz link varies with the interference at any given moment, so these figures are averages of several tests and some runs came out lower.

What I learned

  • Measure before changing. Testing wired and wireless at every point ruled out the routers and pointed at the link, instead of swapping hardware blindly.
  • Test instead of assuming. The channel survey pointed to channel 1 and channel 6 is the one that worked. What matters isn't how many networks there are, but how strong they are and how they overlap.
  • The cable is part of the link too. The failure wasn't a radio problem: it was an indoor cable left in the sun and rain. It's now outdoor Cat 6.
  • Write down the credentials. Not having them meant setting everything up again. They're now saved, and I also took the chance to turn off WPS and leave only WPA2-AES.

A possible next step: moving to 5 GHz equipment, with far less interference, if I can get some line of sight by raising one of the antennas.
